Fighting Climate Change

It all started with the first environmental United Nations' (UN) conference that was held in Stockholm (1972). Here the first declaration of humans' responsibilities for the environment was stated. Since, many conferences* and agreements has taken place towards spreading awareness for nature and humans' impact on it:

  • 1992 Rio de Janeiro - The UN Conference on Environment and Development
    Resulting in: Agenda 21
  • 1997 Kyoto - The UN Framework Convention on Climate Change
    Resulting in: The Kyoto Protocol
  • 2015 Paris - The UN Climate change Conference
    Resulting in: The Paris Agreement

The Danish Climate Act

In 2019 the “Agreement on climate law” was reached that Denmark should have a climate law. This law is known as the Climate Act and entered into force in 2020.

Key Points from the Climate Act

  • 70% reduction of greenhouse emissions by 2030 compared to 1990
  • Climate neutrality should be reached by 2050
  • Working towards keeping global temperature rise below 1.5 degrees Celcius
    *Is part of the Paris Agreement as well

This chart reveals the contrast between two ways of recording Denmark's CO₂ emissions. The steeply declining line shows the official numbers Denmark uses for climate policy—emissions produced within the country's borders.

The other line tells a different story: it tracks the CO₂ emissions that the Danish economy is actually responsible for, including the impact of Danish international transport.

The difference between these two lines is eye-opening. As you compare them, consider what each perspective means for Denmark's climate responsibility, and how the story changes depending on which numbers we choose to focus on.

Which numbers should we focus on?

What is really going on?

Now you've seen why Denmark's total emissions haven't dropped as dramatically as we might expect. Even after decades of climate action, the numbers tell a more complex story than the headlines suggest.

The Climate Law sets a bold goal: a 70% reduction in emissions by 2030, compared to 1990 levels.

But are we close to the goal for 2030?

As you've seen, the answer depends on which numbers we focus on. If we only count emissions produced within Denmark's borders, the target seems within reach and the official story is one of strong progress.

But when we include the emissions our economy is responsible for, those created abroad through imports, travel, and global supply chains, the picture changes. The reductions are far less dramatic, in fact, our true climate footprint has actually increased by 11.9%, remaining much larger than the official numbers used for policies.

Which story should we tell? And which numbers should guide our next steps?
